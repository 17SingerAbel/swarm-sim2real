Abstract— This paper presents a distributed coordination framework for simultaneous multi-target tracking using a wireless sensor network based on discrete-event-system principles. The proposed framework employs a finite-state-machine architecture, where autonomous mobile sensors sequentially process detection and tracking events. Unlike passive tracking approaches that rely on single-sensor monitoring, our proposed strategy implements predictive handover through Extended Kalman Filter based uncertainty propagation, enabling sensors to anticipate target loss events and proactively coordinate auxiliary sensors to reposition along predicted trajectories using navigation guidance. A bidding-based allocation mechanism coordinates sensor assignments by evaluating four competing objectives: network preservation, spatial proximity to handover points, temporal mission feasibility, and estimation uncertainty. The key novelty lies in integrating predictive loss detection with distributed competitive bidding for multi-target scenarios within a reconfigurable sensor network. This approach eliminates a need for centralized control, while maintaining network topology. Extensive simulation results demonstrate tangible improvement in tracking coverage and sensor engagement compared to reactive tracking baselines, across scenarios with varying target speeds and trajectories.  Index Terms— Multi-target tracking, wireless sensor networks, discrete-event-system control, navigation guidance, predictive tracking, distributed coordination
I.  INTRODUCTION UTONOMOUS simultaneous tracking of multiple mobile targets represents a fundamental challenge in distributed robotic systems with applications spanning surveillance [1], search and rescue [2–4], environmental monitoring [5], etc. Dynamic wireless sensor networks (WSNs) offer a scalable solution for effective area coverage, where spatially distributed sensors can cooperate to detect and track targets moving through the monitored region [6,7]. However, maintaining continuous target observation in such environments remains problematic when individual sensors possess limited detection ranges and mobility constraints that restrict their operational displacement from designated home positions [8]. Traditional WSN tracking approaches suggest passive handover strategies where sensors track targets individually until detection is lost and, then, rely on opportunistic encounters with neighboring sensors to reacquire the target [9], [10]. The main contributions of this work are: (1) a predictive handover mechanism using EKF convergence analysis to trigger loss prediction and auxiliary sensor deployment based on guidance theory, (2) a multi-objective bidding mechanism balancing network preservation, spatial proximity, temporal feasibility, and estimation uncertainty for distributed auxiliary sensor allocation, (3) a conflict resolution strategy ensuring discrete event compliance when multiple targets simultaneously require resources, and (4) lightweight EKF-based iso-probability contour generation enabling real-time uncertainty representation for future search operations. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work in multi-target tracking. Section III formulates the problem. Section IV presents the proposed DES architecture. Section V includes simulation results demonstrating tracking performance. Section VI concludes with discussion of key findings and future research directions. II. RELATED WORK A. Predictive Target Tracking Multi-target tracking in distributed sensor networks has evolved from reactive detection-based approaches to predictive frameworks that anticipate tracking failures before they occur. Active tracking algorithms in [17] include provable approximation bounds for handling sensor failures and occlusions, demonstrating that proactive failure detection outperforms reactive recovery. Distributed tracking architectures were extended to autonomous vehicle fleets in [18] and heterogeneous mobile sensing networks in [19], where normalized unused sensing capacity metrics enable automatic load balancing to prevent coverage gaps. Directional-sensor network deployment strategies [20] and dynamic deployment approaches for expanding search areas [21] enable adaptive coverage optimization. Hybrid strategies combining static and mobile sensors [22] with spatiotemporal optimization [23] improve target detection through coordinated sensing and adaptive deployment. The challenge of maintaining continuous observation with limited sensing ranges has also motivated research in collision-free tracking with localization uncertainty [24] and interactive Kalman filtering for multi-vehicle coordination [25]. Distributed state estimation frameworks address tracking loss prediction through modified unscented Kalman filters (KFs) in fading channel environments [26] and simultaneous sensor position refinement [27]. Consensus-based mixture KFs [28] enable tracking of maneuvering targets by predicting conditional dynamics changes, while multi-target information consensus algorithms [29] specifically address tracking loss in non-overlapping sensor fields of view. Recent advances in vision-based reactive planning [30]
incorporate predictive horizons for occlusion handling, and heterogeneous limited-range sensor frameworks [31] use cooperative coverage prediction to prevent tracking gaps. However, these approaches primarily focus on state estimation quality rather than explicitly predicting when and where current trackers will lose targets, creating opportunities for proactive sensor repositioning based on loss prediction rather than reactive reacquisition. B. Distributed Coordination and Task Allocation Task allocation in multi-robot systems has transitioned from centralized optimization to distributed mechanisms enabling scalable coordination without global knowledge. Group-based distributed auction algorithms [32] address time-window constraints and conflict resolution through competitive bidding, while factor graph formulations with max-sum algorithms [33] enable fully decentralized task allocation. Resource-oriented auction mechanisms [34] consider energy constraints and limited communication ranges, demonstrating that distributed bidding outperforms centralized allocation in communication-constrained environments. Active-vision-based multi-sensor surveillance systems [35] demonstrate effective coordination for multi-target monitoring in dynamic environments. Network topology preservation has also received attention through information space feedback control [36] and multi-robot coordination with task precedence relationships [37]. Conflict resolution, when multiple tasks compete for limited resources, has been addressed through metaheuristic optimization [38] and hierarchical FSMs [39]. Connectivity recovery methods [40] use FSM-based distributed coordination to identify critical nodes and coordinate network self-healing, while consensus-based bundle algorithms [41] enable task allocation in teams with severely limited communication. Reconfigurable coordination frameworks [42] provide virtual coordinator architectures for discrete-event control systems, enabling dynamic role transitions without centralized supervisors. However, current task-allocation methods typically optimize a single objective or use fixed-priority schemes, lacking multi-objective frameworks that simultaneously balance network topology preservation, spatial mission costs, temporal feasibility, and information quality which are all critical for maintaining persistent tracking coverage while minimizing network disruption. C. Target Pursuit Guidance theory has been successfully adapted to robotic systems for target interception and pursuit-evasion scenarios. Navigation-guidance-based approaches [43] and augmented proportional navigation techniques [44] have demonstrated effective robotic pursuit of moving objects. Building on these foundations, proportional navigation guidance (PNG) has

Optimal guidance strategies for UAV swarms [46] integrate pure pursuit laws with formation maintenance, while bio-inspired cooperative hunting behaviors [47] demonstrate mixed guidance approaches using Markov decision processes for multi-agent coordination. Pursuit-evasion frameworks have been developed for multi-robot systems with limited sensing [48], incorporating evolutionary robotics approaches for decentralized control. The integration of guidance theory with distributed sensor coordination for predictive handover - where sensors position themselves at predicted handover points before current trackers lose targets - remains largely unexplored in WSN multi-target tracking applications. This architecture enables efficient coordination by processing discrete tracking events (target detections, predicted losses, handover requests), while minimizing computational overhead during periods of tracking. Each sensor operates according to a finite-state machine (Fig. 2) with six states S = {IDLE, DETECTING, TRACKING, REPOSITIONING, RETURNING_HOME}. State transitions are event-driven: • IDLE → DETECTING occurs upon target detection within range • DETECTING → TRACKING occurs when fewer than two sensors are currently tracking the target (dual-tracker configuration has available slots) • IDLE → REPOSITIONING occurs when an idle sensor wins bidding for a handover call • REPOSITIONING → TRACKING occurs upon reaching the predicted handover point and successfully acquiring the target • TRACKING → RETURNING_HOME occurs when the sensor loses target detection • REPOSITIONING → RETURNING_HOME occurs when the sensor cannot acquire the target at the handover point • Any state → RETURNING_HOME occurs when the sensor exceeds the displacement threshold 𝑑&'( without active mission.

D. Performance Summary  The two scenarios collectively demonstrated four key system capabilities. First, the conflict resolution mechanism successfully manages simultaneous multi-target demands through min-max optimization. Second, the dual-tracker configuration provides robustness against individual sensor failures or transient detection losses, as evidenced by continuous tracking despite overlapping coverage areas and high sensor utilization. Third, the predictive handover framework scales upward effectively while maintaining tracking continuity under progressive velocity increases. Fourth, the bidding mechanism’s network preservation component ensures sensors remain within displacement constraints, preserving network topology for subsequent tracking missions. VI. CONCLUSIONS This paper presents a distributed coordination framework for multi-target tracking in wireless sensor networks based on DES principles and predictive guidance theory. The proposed approach transforms passive tracking, where sensors react to target loss events, into active swarm coordination where sensors proactively position themselves at predicted handover points before current trackers lose detection. Results from simulated experiments validate the effectiveness of predictive handover, demonstrating 122% improvement in tracking coverage (80.4 vs 36.2 time units) and 117% increase in sensor engagement (52% vs 24% network utilization) compared to reactive tracking baselines. The system achieved 10 successful predictive handovers from 13 handover calls (76.9% success rate), maintaining continuous multi-target tracking through proactive sensor repositioning. Active coordination engaged 28 percentage points more of the sensor network and doubled detection events, enabling persistent tracking across trajectory discontinuities that caused complete tracking loss in reactive approaches.  The integration of predictive handover, competitive bidding, and conflict resolution enables proactive sensor coordination while maintaining network topology. Simulation results demonstrate substantial improvements in tracking coverage and sensor engagement compared to reactive approaches, validating the framework's effectiveness across diverse target scenarios.
