Abstract— This paper presents a distributed coordination framework for simultaneous multi-target tracking using a wireless sensor network based on discrete-event-system principles.
The proposed framework employs a finite-state-machine architecture, where autonomous mobile sensors sequentially process detection and tracking events.
Unlike passive tracking approaches that rely on single-sensor monitoring, our proposed strategy implements predictive handover through Extended Kalman Filter based uncertainty propagation, enabling sensors to anticipate target loss events and proactively coordinate auxiliary sensors to reposition along predicted trajectories using navigation guidance.
A bidding-based allocation mechanism coordinates sensor assignments by evaluating four competing objectives: network preservation, spatial proximity to handover points, temporal mission feasibility, and estimation uncertainty.
The key novelty lies in integrating predictive loss detection with distributed competitive bidding for multi-target scenarios within a reconfigurable sensor network.
Index Terms— Multi-target tracking, wireless sensor networks, discrete-event-system control, navigation guidance, predictive tracking, distributed coordination I.
INTRODUCTION UTONOMOUS simultaneous tracking of multiple mobile targets represents a fundamental challenge in distributed robotic systems with applications spanning surveillance [1], search and rescue [2–4], environmental monitoring [5], etc.
Dynamic wireless sensor networks (WSNs) offer a scalable solution for effective area coverage, where spatially distributed sensors can cooperate to detect and track targets moving through the monitored region [6,7].
Traditional WSN tracking approaches suggest passive handover strategies where sensors track targets individually until detection is lost and, then, rely on opportunistic encounters with neighboring sensors to reacquire the target [9], [10].
A passive approach may fail to leverage the distributed sensing and communication capabilities inherent in networked systems, treating the WSN as a collection of independent agents rather than a coordinated system [12].
This paper presents a novel distributed coordination framework that implements predictive active handover, where sensors use Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) based uncertainty propagation to anticipate tracker loss events and proactively position auxiliary sensors along predicted target trajectories using guidance-theory principles [15], [16].
The proposed discrete-event-system (DES) architecture coordinates autonomous sensors through finite-state machines (FSMs), enabling dynamic role transitions between detecting and tracking based on target behavior and network conditions.

Predictive Target Tracking Multi-target tracking in distributed sensor networks has evolved from reactive detection-based approaches to predictive frameworks that anticipate tracking failures before they occur.
Distributed tracking architectures were extended to autonomous vehicle fleets in [18] and heterogeneous mobile sensing networks in [19], where normalized unused sensing capacity metrics enable automatic load balancing to prevent coverage gaps.
Distributed state estimation frameworks address tracking loss prediction through modified unscented Kalman filters (KFs) in fading channel environments [26] and simultaneous sensor position refinement [27].
Distributed Coordination and Task Allocation Task allocation in multi-robot systems has transitioned from centralized optimization to distributed mechanisms enabling scalable coordination without global knowledge.
Group-based distributed auction algorithms [32] address time-window constraints and conflict resolution through competitive bidding, while factor graph formulations with max-sum algorithms [33] enable fully decentralized task allocation.
Resource-oriented auction mechanisms [34] consider energy constraints and limited communication ranges, demonstrating that distributed bidding outperforms centralized allocation in communication-constrained environments.
Conflict resolution, when multiple tasks compete for limited resources, has been addressed through metaheuristic optimization [38] and hierarchical FSMs [39].
Connectivity recovery methods [40] use FSM-based distributed coordination to identify critical nodes and coordinate network self-healing, while consensus-based bundle algorithms [41] enable task allocation in teams with severely limited communication.
Reconfigurable coordination frameworks [42] provide virtual coordinator architectures for discrete-event control systems, enabling dynamic role transitions without centralized supervisors.
